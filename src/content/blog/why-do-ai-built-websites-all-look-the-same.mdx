AI builds the most likely design, which is the average of every website it learned from. Give it no direction and you get the defaults: a centered hero, three feature cards, rounded corners everywhere, and a purple gradient. The fix isn't a better prompt. It's a design doc the AI reads before every change.

A commenter on my last LinkedIn post put it bluntly: "The problem with AI websites - they all look the same." He's right about most of them. Here's why it happens, and the doc I use so mine doesn't.

## Why do AI websites all look the same?

Because the AI fills every decision you didn't make with the most common answer. Stack enough of those together and you get a look you've seen a hundred times:

- Everything in a box, often boxes inside boxes
- Rounded corners on everything, usually 8 to 12 pixels
- Inter for the text, plus one more sans-serif for headings
- Slate gray with a single indigo, violet, or blue accent
- A centered hero, then three features in a row, then a call to action
- Soft gradients and glowing dots for decoration
- Copy like "empower your journey"

None of these are wrong on their own. Together, they're the look of a site nobody made a decision about.

I've been producing music since the late '90s, and producers have a name for this: the preset sound. Load the same factory synth patch as everyone else, and your track sounds like everyone else's. AI defaults are the preset.

## Is that the AI's fault?

No. The AI does what it's told. If you don't tell it much, you get the average.

A prompt like "make it modern and clean" doesn't help, because modern and clean *is* the average. What changes the result is giving the AI specific decisions it has to follow, every time, in writing.

## What goes in a design doc?

My site has one: `docs/design-principles.md`, about 250 lines in the repo. It opens with one idea: the site is the proof. My audience builds things, and a site that looks generated would quietly undercut the method I'm writing about.

Here's what's in it:

- **A list of generic tells, each with the rule that replaces it.** Rounded corners everywhere becomes "sharp by default," with a 3px radius. Boxes inside boxes become thin divider lines and white space. A centered layout becomes left-aligned, with uneven spacing on purpose.
- **Three fonts, each with a job.** Satoshi for headings, Sentient (a serif) for reading, and Commit Mono for labels, buttons, and code. The doc sums it up as "serif = read, sans = state, mono = operate." The serif body alone breaks the all-sans look most AI sites have.
- **One loud color.** Orange (#DD3300) for the call to action, and never for body text. Everything else is slate.
- **A few signature details, used sparingly.** A faint dot grid behind every page, path-style labels like `~/blog`, and a file-tree footer.
- **A "do not build" list.** No fake terminal hero, no typewriter headline, no matrix rain, no glitch text. Neo can keep the matrix rain. The doc puts it this way: "restraint reads as senior; excess reads as costume."
- **Non-negotiables.** Accessible contrast, full keyboard navigation, reduced-motion support, and a Lighthouse score of 95 or higher. Fast is part of the design.

## How do you get the AI to actually follow it?

You make reading it part of the AI's instructions. Claude Code reads a `CLAUDE.md` file in the project before it does anything. Mine points straight at the design doc:

```md
## Design

All design and styling decisions follow docs/design-principles.md ...
Read it before any styling, component, or page work, and build against it
rather than reaching for defaults. If a default and that doc disagree, the doc wins.
```

That last line does most of the work. When the AI has to choose between its default and my rule, the rule wins.

The second half is review. Every change shows up as a diff before it goes live, so when something drifts back toward the defaults, I catch it and point to the rule.

## What does restraint look like in practice?

Last week I wanted an animated background. Something techy and low-key, just enough to impress.

I asked my AI about it, and it pushed back by quoting my own design doc at me. The dot grid is "the *only* sanctioned background treatment." The page already has one moving element, the ring around the email signup, and a second one would compete with it. And animated backgrounds are one of the most common AI-site looks right now, which was the whole point of this post.

It was right. Annoyingly right. I kept the dot grid. (A cursor-reactive version of it is parked for later, but it has to earn its way in.)

That's the real value of the doc. It isn't only instructions for the AI. It holds me to decisions I already made, even when I'm the one trying to break them.

## How do you write one for your own site?

Start small. A page of real decisions beats ten pages of vibes.

1. **List the generic patterns you want to avoid.** Look at a few AI-built sites and write down what they have in common.
2. **Pick one signature.** One idea that makes the site yours. Mine is "developer-native": mono labels, a visible grid, and real code as content.
3. **Choose two or three fonts and give each a job.** Reading, headings, and labels.
4. **Pick one loud color**, and say where it's allowed.
5. **Write a "do not build" list.** It's often more useful than the "do" list.
6. **Point your AI at it.** `CLAUDE.md` for Claude Code, `AGENTS.md` for Codex and other tools, or your editor's rules file. Tell it the doc wins over defaults.

**Not sure what you like yet?** Collect a folder of screenshots from sites you admire and have your AI study them. Ask it what they have in common: the fonts, the spacing, the colors, and the layout patterns. That gives you a starting point for your own rules.

Just don't stop there. Keep what you like, change what you don't, and make it yours. Copy someone else's look and you've only traded one default for another.

Then keep it current. When you make a design call in a conversation, add it to the doc so the next session knows.

## When does this not matter?

For a quick landing page to test an idea, or an internal tool only your team sees, the defaults are fine. Spend the time on the idea instead.

It matters when the site *is* the pitch: a personal brand, a business, or anything where looking like everyone else costs you trust.

## Quick questions

### Can't I just prompt "make it unique"?

You can, but you'll get a random change that doesn't hold. The next session starts from the defaults again. A doc in the project carries your decisions into every session.

### Do I need a designer?

You need opinions, not a job title. A design doc is a list of decisions. If you can say what you like, what you don't, and why, you can write one.

### Does this work with Cursor or Codex?

Yes. Any AI coding tool that reads an instructions file can follow a design doc. Codex reads `AGENTS.md`, Claude Code reads `CLAUDE.md`, and Cursor has its own rules files.

### How long should a design doc be?

Mine is about 250 lines. Start with one page and grow it as you make decisions.
